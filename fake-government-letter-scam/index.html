What a fake government message often looks like

A letter, email or text that seems to come from the tax office, the police or a benefits agency. It says “you are owed a tax refund”, “pay this fine today” or “your benefits are suspended”, and asks you to act through a link, a number or a payment. Common signs: a refund you didn't expect, a request for card details to receive money, a fine you can't place, a very short deadline, threats of arrest, payment by gift card, crypto or to an unfamiliar account, and a sender address that doesn't match the authority. Real authorities send letters with deadlines too, so check rather than guess.

What to do next

Don't click links, call the number in the message, open attachments or pay until you have checked. Find the authority's contact details yourself, from its official website or a letter you know is real, and ask whether the message is theirs. Real authorities usually send decisions by post or to your official online account, and a real tax refund usually goes to the bank account they already have on file. They don't usually ask for card details by email or text, or for payment by gift card or crypto.

Still unsure? Ask CalmClick a follow-up question or show the explanation to someone you trust. Checking should be quick.

A real notice, such as a tax assessment or a fine, can have a deadline printed on it. Don't leave it unopened. If it is real, act by the date given or ask the authority what your options are. If you can't tell and a deadline is close, contact the authority or someone you trust right away.

If you already paid or shared details

Stay calm: this happens to many careful people. Call your bank using the number on your card or your bank's official website and tell them it may be a scam payment; the sooner, the better, though a payment cannot be stopped in every case. Entered a password or tax login? Change it on the official website. You can also report it to the police. This is general information, not financial, tax or legal advice.
